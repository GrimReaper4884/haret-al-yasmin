# Damascus Lane Life (1920s–1930s): Research Notes for Haret al-Yasmin

These notes collect historical and ethnographic detail for writing original characters, quests and dialogue. TV dramas were deliberately left out as sources. The marks used below:
- **[S]**: backed by the source linked in that section.
- **[G]**: general, widely attested knowledge that we did not check against a fetched source. Confirm it before using it as a "fact" in UI text.
- **[Game]**: a design suggestion, not history.

---

## 1. The hara as a social unit

- **Quarter vs. lane.** Old Damascus was divided into quarters (*ḥayy* / *maḥalla*, e.g. al-Shaghour, al-Qaymariyya, al-Midan). Each quarter held many lanes (*ḥāra*) and dead-end alleys (*zuqāq*, *ʿaṭfa*). Some quarters were mostly of one faith. In 1936, Shaghour was recorded as entirely Muslim, and Qaymariyya had 5,817 Muslims and 241 Christians. Bab Touma was the main Christian area, and there was also a Jewish quarter. [S: [Al-Shaghur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shaghur,_Damascus), [Qaymariyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaymariyya)]
- **Gates and night closure.** The city wall had seven classic gates (Bab Touma, Bab al-Salam, Bab al-Faradis, Bab al-Jabiya, Bab al-Saghir, Bab Kisan, Bab Sharqi), and a traveller's account says they were shut at sunset [S: [Emarat al-Youm](https://www.emaratalyoum.com/online/follow-ups/2019-03-28-1.1196933), [travel blog](https://blog.mailasail.com/vascodagama/posts/2010/7/5/67-damascus)]. Many lanes and alleys also had their own wooden gate that was barred at night and watched by a night guard (*nāṭūr* or *ḥāris*) [G]. Hasan al-Kharrat, the famous qabaday of Shaghour, was officially the quarter's **night watchman** and also guarded its orchards [S: [Hasan al-Kharrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasan_al-Kharrat)].
- **The mukhtar (مختار).** Ottoman reforms (muhtar elections from 1829, then the 1864 Vilayet Law) created this headman post for quarters and villages. In a city his work was mostly paperwork: registering residents, births and deaths, certifying identity, passing on announcements, and helping with taxes and public order. He was the state's man in the lane. [S: [Muhtar (Küre)](https://kureansiklopedi.com/en/detay/muhtar-2), [Muktar](https://wiki.krisyotam.com/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic_2026-03/Muktar)] In Damascus the mukhtar also **appointed the Ramadan night-caller (musaḥḥarātī)**, who knew every household by name [S: [Raseef22](https://raseef22.net/english/article/1096868-almusaharati-and-the-hakawati-beloved-dad-damascene-ramadan-traditions-that-died)].
- **The qabaday (قبضاي).** He was the informal leader of a quarter's young men. He took up grievances and defended the quarter's honour against criminals and against qabadays from other quarters. Philip Khoury called him an "upholder of Arab traditions" and a "guardian of popular culture." He was admired for strength and for **protecting the poor and minorities**, he usually had little schooling, and he was typically tied to a notable family whose political support he delivered [S: [Hasan al-Kharrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasan_al-Kharrat)]. A comparable Beirut source describes his code. It includes *nakhwe* (helping before being asked, at fires, weddings and funerals), *ṣulḥa* (reconciliation: visiting both parties several times before brokering peace), organising celebrations and funerals, and holding court in the coffeehouse. He also observed the household's privacy: calling "yalla, yalla" before entering a courtyard, and never staring at a woman who came to him with a request. He dressed in good cloth and carried a stick or dagger, mostly for show [S: [LanguageWave, Beirut's qabadayat](https://languagewave.com/2020/04/02/episode-19-beiruts-qabadayat-valiant-men/)]. The academic reference is Khoury, "Syrian Urban Politics in Transition: The Quarters of Damascus during the French Mandate," *IJMES* 16:4 (1984).
- **Notables (al-aʿyān, al-wujahāʾ).** These were landowning, scholarly and merchant families who acted as patrons of a quarter. In Shaghour, for example, the al-Bakri family used the qabaday as its link to ordinary residents [S: [Hasan al-Kharrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasan_al-Kharrat); background in Khoury, *Urban Notables and Arab Nationalism*, CUP 1983].
- **The imam and the shaykh.** The imam led prayer at the lane mosque or *zāwiya*, taught children Qur'an in the *kuttāb*, and was asked to witness contracts, read the Fatiha at engagements and mediate in quarrels [G].
- **Settling disputes.** The usual path ran from the elders, to the qabaday's *ṣulḥa*, to the mukhtar, and only then to the courts (the sharia court for marriage, inheritance and debts; Mandate courts for crimes). Reputation (*sumʿa*), honour (*sharaf*) and a man's word (*kilme*) were the real currency. Being publicly shamed in front of the lane hurt more than a fine [G].

## 2. Daily life and trades

- **Guilds.** Every craft had a master, the *shaykh al-kār*, appointed by a judge's deed. One late-Ottoman count lists about 435 registered crafts [S: [Syrian Heritage, saddle makers](https://syrian-heritage.org/the-last-master-of-the-saddle-makers)]. The key source is the *Qāmūs al-ṣināʿāt al-shāmiyya* (Dictionary of Damascene Crafts, c.1890–1906), indexed at [Crafts of Syria](https://craftsofsyria.uvic.ca/damascus/).
- **Furn (public oven).** Each lane's oven baked flatbread and also baked households' own trays (kibbeh, ka'ak, Eid ma'amoul) for a small fee [G]. [Game]: the oven is a natural quest hub, with errands like "carry Umm X's tray to the furn."
- **Spice and perfume sellers.** These were concentrated in **Souq al-Bzouriyeh**, south of the Umayyad Mosque. It sold spices, perfumes, sweets, dried fruit and laurel soap, and was lined with khans such as Khan Asʿad Pasha [S: [Al-Buzuriyah Souq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Buzuriyah_Souq)].
- **Coppersmiths (naḥḥās).** They beat large pots and cauldrons for weddings, schools and the army, and tinners (*mbayyiḍ*) re-coated pans [S: [Copper Beaters](https://craftsofsyria.uvic.ca/damascus-copper-beaters/), [Tinners](https://craftsofsyria.uvic.ca/damascus-tinners/)].
- **Tarboosh makers.** In 1928 Freya Stark saw felt pressed over "a brass press heated with a primus stove." Separate **tarboosh polishers** re-shaped worn fezzes [S: [Tarboosh Makers](https://craftsofsyria.uvic.ca/damascus-makers-of-tarbooshes/), [Tarboosh Polishers](https://craftsofsyria.uvic.ca/tarboosh-polisher/)].
- **Tailors** worked alongside weavers of silk and brocade and embroiderers using silver wire, since Damascus was famous for its textiles [S: [Tailors](https://craftsofsyria.uvic.ca/damascus-tailors/), [Brocade Makers](https://craftsofsyria.uvic.ca/damascus-brocade-makers/)].
- **Drink sellers.** The *ʿarʾsūs* (liquorice) seller carried a large brass jug on his back and clacked brass cups to attract customers. Tamarind (*tamr hindī*) and *sūs* were Ramadan staples [S: [JPost Ramadan foods](https://jpost.com/middle-east/article-888089)]. A *saqqā* (water carrier) delivered from the city's channels and fountains [G].
- **Coffeehouse and hakawati.** At **al-Nawfara café** behind the Umayyad Mosque, storytellers read epics such as *ʿAntar ibn Shaddād* and *al-Zīr Sālim* from manuscripts and banged a stick or sword on the table at the climax. The storyteller wore a tarboosh, sherwal and a colourful waistcoat [S: [Raseef22](https://raseef22.net/english/article/1096868-almusaharati-and-the-hakawati-beloved-dad-damascene-ramadan-traditions-that-died), [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/mena/the-storyteller-of-damascus-is-silenced-by-war-1.1344867)]. **Shadow puppets** of painted, stretched animal skin were also performed in coffee shops [S: [Shadow Puppeteers](https://craftsofsyria.uvic.ca/damascus-shadow-puppeteer/)].
- **Hammam.** Bathers moved through cold, warm and hot rooms. Men and women used the bath at **separate hours**. Brides bathed before the wedding, and grooms went with friends on the wedding morning. Hammam Nur al-Din in the Bzouriyeh souq dates to about 1160 [S: [Hammams in Damascus](https://www.inyourpocket.com/syria/hammams-in-damascus_81183f), [Hammam Nur al-Din](https://syrian-treasures.com/en/hammam-nur-aldin/)].
- **Souqs.**
  - *Souq al-Hamidiyya* is the covered main street from the Citadel to the Umayyad Mosque, rebuilt with an iron roof after an 1884 fire. In 1898 it held cloth, clothing and confectioners selling ices [S: [Al-Hamidiyah Souq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hamidiyah_Souq)]. Bakdash, the stretchy *booza* ice-cream shop thickened with mastic and sahlab, is dated to 1885 or 1895 [S: [Bakdash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakdash_(ice_cream_parlor))].
  - *Souq al-Bzouriyeh* sold spices, sweets and soap [S].
  - The *Souq al-Ṣāgha* (goldsmiths) and the medieval pearl market (*Sūq al-luʾluʾ*) lay south of the Great Mosque [S: [Pearl Merchants](https://craftsofsyria.uvic.ca/damascus-pearl-merchants/)].
  - *Khan al-Harir* (1573) is a merchants' khan whose name means "silk" [S: [Syrian Treasures](https://syrian-treasures.com/en/khan-al-harir-2/)].
  - *Souq Midhat Pasha* runs along Straight Street and sold cloth and household goods [G].

## 3. Domestic life

- **The courtyard house (bayt ʿarabī).** The street wall was blank and plain. A **bent entrance passage (dahlīz)** stopped passers-by from seeing inside [S: [Met, Damascus Room](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-damascus-room)]. Inside was a stone-paved courtyard (*arḍ al-diyār*) with a marble **fountain (baḥra)**, citrus trees (bitter orange, lemon), jasmine, vines and roses [S: [Syrian Guides](https://syrianguides.com/why-damascus-hides-its-most-beautiful-homes/), [Beit al-Sibai](https://syrian-treasures.com/en/beit-al-sibai/)]. The **iwan (līwān)** was a high arched room open to the courtyard, usually facing north so it stayed shaded for summer sitting. Reception halls (*qāʿa*) had painted wood ceilings. The ground floor was used in summer and the warmer upper floor in winter [S: [AlAqqad House](https://syrian-treasures.com/en/alaqqad-house/)].
- **Rooftops and women's networks.** Flat roofs were used for drying apricots and making tomato paste, for laundry and for summer sleeping. Women visited across roofs and through connecting doors, met at the hammam and held regular morning visits (*istiqbāl*) [G].
- **Weddings** [G except where marked]:
  1. Women of the groom's family visit candidate brides, often at the hammam or at home.
  2. *Ṭulbe*: the men's formal request, over coffee.
  3. Reading the **Fatiha**, then agreeing the **mahr**. The mahr has two parts: an advance (*muqaddam*) paid in gold coins and jewellery, and a deferred part (*muʾakhkhar*). It is written by the sharia judge in the **katb al-kitāb** before witnesses. Gold is still the preferred dowry unit in Syria today [S: [Enab Baladi](https://english.enabbaladi.net/archives/2023/02/syrians-request-daughters-dowries-in-gold/amp/)].
  4. The **jihāz** (trousseau) is carried through the lanes on display: inlaid mother-of-pearl chest, copper pots, embroidered linens, bedding.
  5. The bride's **hammam day**, then the **henna night** (*laylat al-ḥinna*) with women singing.
  6. The **zaffeh**, a procession with drums, sword-and-shield dancers (*ʿarāḍa*) and ululation (*zaghārīt*), taking the bride to the groom's house.
- **Ramadan.** In the week before, flags and lanterns were strung through the lanes. On sighting the crescent, families gave meat and sugar to the poor. A cannon was fired before Maghrib for iftar and again for suhoor. The **musaḥḥarātī** drummed through the lanes calling "Wake up for suhoor!" with children joining in, and at Eid he got an *ʿīdiyye* of money and a mixed plate of food. Free printed **imsakiyye** timetables were handed out [S: [Raseef22](https://raseef22.net/english/article/1096868-almusaharati-and-the-hakawati-beloved-dad-damascene-ramadan-traditions-that-died), [Iftar Cannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iftar_Cannon)]. Typical foods were tamariyye (a date pastry), *nāʿem* bread drizzled with syrup, liquorice and tamarind drinks [S: [JPost](https://jpost.com/middle-east/article-888089)], and yoghurt dishes at the start of the month [S: [Euronews](https://euronews.com/culture/2026/02/28/ramadan-rituals-foods-and-customs-that-shape-the-holy-month-of-fasting-in-syria)].
- **Food** [G]: *kaʿk* (sesame bread rings), *maʿmoul* (date or nut-filled Eid cookies pressed in carved wooden moulds), *sahlab* (hot orchid-root milk in winter), *booza*, *kibbeh*, *fatteh*, *mulukhiyye*, *makdous* (stuffed pickled aubergine), apricot leather (*ʾamar al-dīn*), and *mabrūma* and *barāzeq* sweets.

## 4. Money and valuables

- **Currency.** The **Syrian pound** (*līra sūriyye*) was created on 31 March 1920 by High Commissioner Gouraud's Order No. 129. It replaced the Egyptian pound, was fixed at **20 French francs**, and was divided into **100 piastres (qirsh)**. From 1924 it was issued by the Banque de Syrie et du Grand-Liban as the "Syro-Lebanese" pound, in notes of 1 to 500 livres. Piastre coins and small notes (½, 1, 2½, 5, 10, 25 piastres) were in use [S: [Lebanese-Syrian pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese-Syrian_pound), [Syrian pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_pound), [Numista](https://numista.com/245792)]. In 1921 the new money still competed with "a plethora of existing currencies" and only became sole tender by the mid-1920s [S: [StashOfCoins](https://stashofcoins.com/coin/78639-syria-½-piastre-1921)].
- **Ottoman gold.** The Ottoman gold lira (*līra dhahab ʿuthmāliyye*, nicknamed by sultan, e.g. *ḥamīdiyye* or *rashādiyye*) and the English gold sovereign stayed the preferred **store of value and dowry unit** long after 1920 [G]. Paper money was often distrusted after wartime inflation [G].
- **Jewellery for dowries** [G]: gold bracelets (*asāwir*, *mabrūm*), a gold coin necklace or headband, earrings, rings with **pearls (luʾluʾ)**, and for rich families diamonds (*almās*) set in silver-topped gold. Damascus jewellers worked in silver and gold, and many of the best were Christian craftsmen near the Umayyad Mosque. In 1928 Freya Stark saw them "lock their goods in iron safes over night" [S: [Jewellers](https://craftsofsyria.uvic.ca/damascus-jewellers/)].
- **Keeping valuables** [G]: hidden in a locked mother-of-pearl chest (*ṣandūq ṣadaf*), sewn into cushions or a belt, under a floor tile, or left with a trusted merchant or notable. Gold worn on the body was a woman's own property and her safety net.
- **Credit** [G]: shopkeepers kept a tab book (*daftar*). Moneychangers (*ṣarrāf*) worked near the souqs. Pawning gold with a jeweller or moneylender was common, interest was religiously frowned upon, and debt to an outsider was a family shame.
- **Price scale** [Game, approximate]: bread costs a few piastres, a coffee 1 to 2 piastres, a hammam visit 5 to 10, a tarboosh 20 to 50, a gold lira about 1 to 2 Syrian pounds, and a modest mahr 20 to 100 gold liras. These figures are tuned for gameplay and are not documented.

## 5. Crime and justice

- **Inside the lane** [G]: a theft was first handled by the household, the elders and the qabaday. The young men searched, outsiders were questioned at the gate, and stolen goods were often quietly returned through a go-between to avoid scandal. Restitution plus a public apology before the elders was the usual outcome. Being publicly known as a thief, or banned from the coffeehouse, was the real punishment.
- **Oaths**: swearing on the Qur'an or by God (*wallāh al-ʿaẓīm*), or "by my moustache / my honour," settled many disputes. A false oath was considered disgraceful [G].
- **The state.** Under the Mandate, the **police were commanded by Syrian officers** with French technical advisers, while the **gendarmerie was led by French officers**. Gendarmes, mobile guards and rural guards kept order, and police also suppressed nationalist demonstrations (Damascus, April 1922) [S: [Damascus Univ. abstract](https://www.damascusuniversity.edu.sy/human/downloads/files/1701901365_الملخص%20-عربي%20إنكليزي.pdf), [Army of the Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_of_the_Levant), [UCA timeline](https://uca.edu/politicalscience/dadm-project/middle-eastnorth-africapersian-gulf-region/french-syria-1919-1946)]. Residents generally preferred to keep the police out of lane affairs [G].
- **1925–26.** Qabadays such as al-Kharrat led fighters during the Great Syrian Revolt. French shelling burned the area south of the Citadel, which afterwards became known as **al-Ḥarīqa** ("the Conflagration"), and later shelled al-Midan [S: [Al-Hariqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hariqa), [Great Syrian Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Syrian_Revolt)]. See section 9 before using this in the game.

## 6. Clothing

- **Men** [S: [Palestine Heritage](https://palestineheritage.org/?p=4974), [Britannica: tarboosh](https://www.britannica.com/topic/tarboosh); otherwise G]:
  - **Qumbāz**: an ankle-length front-wrapped robe in striped cotton or Syrian silk (*alāja*), cinched with a sash (*zunnār* or *shāl*).
  - **Sherwāl**: baggy trousers, black for the qabaday style, with a short **ṣidriyye** waistcoat and jacket.
  - **Ṭarbūsh**: red felt with a black tassel. Merchants and clerks wore it plain, and scholars wrapped it with a white turban (*laffe*).
  - **ʿAbāʾa**: a camel-hair or wool cloak. The **labbāde** was a felt skullcap worn under the tarboosh or by labourers. The **kūfiyye/ḥaṭṭa** was more rural and Bedouin, worn in town by carters and villagers.
  - **Shoes**: red or yellow leather slippers (*bābūj*, *kundara*), and wooden clogs (*ʾabqāb*) in the hammam.
  - By the 1930s, educated young men wore European suits with the tarboosh.
- **Women** [S: [Moubayed](https://en.samimoubayed.com/?p=379), [Al-Jadid: Veiling of the City](https://aljadid.com/content/veiling-city), [Dressed with Distinction](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/52489400); otherwise G]:
  - Outdoors, the black **mlāye/mulāʾa** wrap covered the body, with a thin black face veil (*mandīl* or *ṭarḥa*). Unveiling began in Damascus in the 1920s among the urban elite, and the first notable public unveiling is linked to the 1922 Crane visit.
  - Indoors, women wore bright silk dresses and jackets (*qumbāz*), embroidered scarves, and gold coins and jewellery.
- **Children** [G]: boys wore a small qumbaz or shirt and sherwal with a skullcap or small tarboosh. Girls wore colourful dresses and a light scarf when older. Barefoot or clogs.
- **Materials**: Damascus brocade, striped silk-cotton *alāja*, wool, felt and linen. Colours were indigo, madder red, black, cream and saffron yellow [S: [Dyers](https://craftsofsyria.uvic.ca/damascus-dyers/), [Madder-root](https://craftsofsyria.uvic.ca/damascus-cultivators-and-processors-of-madder-root/)].

## 8. Story-hook ideas (original)

1. **The missing dowry box.** Three days before a wedding, the bride's gold liras and pearl earrings vanish from the mother-of-pearl chest. Clues lead to the hammam, the tinner and a jealous cousin. The resolution is a quiet return through *ṣulḥa*, not the police.
2. **The outside moneylender.** The baker pawned his oven tools to a *ṣarrāf* from another quarter. Players earn the money through deliveries before the deadline or win the qabaday's support.
3. **Lane rivalry.** Youths from the neighbouring lane challenge ours at Eid: a stick-dance (*ʿarāḍa*) contest, a kite fight on the roofs, or a *zaffeh* drumming duel.
4. **The dry fountain.** The courtyard *baḥra* stops flowing. Trace the water channel, find a blockage, then convince the water warden and neighbours to share the repair cost.
5. **The lost permit.** The coppersmith's guild deed from the *shaykh al-kār* is lost. Without it he can't sell in the souq. Search the khan and the coffeehouse.
6. **The musaharati's drum.** In Ramadan the night-caller's drum is stolen, so nobody will wake for suhoor. Recover it and help him do his rounds, collecting *ʿīdiyye* at Eid.
7. **The hakawati's missing chapter.** Pages of the *ʿAntar* manuscript are scattered across the lane. Collect them before the evening performance.
8. **The night gate.** The gate wasn't barred last night and the guard is suspected. Investigate and clear his name.
9. **The jihaz parade.** Organise the trousseau procession: borrow a cart, fetch pots from the coppersmith, polish the groom's tarboosh.
10. **Jasmine harvest.** Gather jasmine and bitter oranges for blossom water and jam for a visiting family, as a time-limited event.

## 9. Sensitivities (kids' game, Levantine players)

- **Religion**: show prayer, mosques, churches and Ramadan respectfully. Never use sacred text, the call to prayer or the Qur'an as game items or jokes. Lanes can be mixed (Muslim, Christian, and historically Jewish) with good-neighbour relations, which is historically grounded. Avoid sectarian villains.
- **The Mandate and violence**: do not depict French soldiers killing people, bombardments, the 1925 burning of al-Hariqa, or rebels as playable combat. Refer to the era lightly (currency, a gendarme who is an off-screen "patrol"). No guns, and no daggers used on people. The qabaday solves problems through strength of character, wrestling-style challenges and *ṣulḥa*. Given the recent war, keep destruction imagery out entirely.
- **Real names**: don't use real families (al-Bakri, al-ʿAẓm), real qabadays or real politicians as characters. Invent names, and keep invented quarters fictional ("Haret al-Yasmin").
- **Gender roles**: reflect period dress and gendered spaces, but give women agency as traders of embroidery and preserves, hammam networks, matchmakers, and wise elders who solve mysteries. No honour violence, no forced-marriage plots, no child marriage. Brides and grooms should be clearly adult, and weddings should be shown as community festivals. Make veiling an optional style rather than a gameplay rule.
- **Crime**: thieves are misguided, not evil. Prefer restitution and apology over punishment. No public humiliation mechanics aimed at players.
- **Substances**: the coffeehouse can have coffee, tea and backgammon, with no argileh, smoking or alcohol (Roblox policy).
- **TV likeness**: avoid recreating well-known drama characters, catch-phrases, theme music or the iconic "Bab al-Hara" framing. Keep all names and plots original.
- **Language**: have a native Damascene speaker review it. Keep insults to the mild list above, and never mock accents or religious phrases.
